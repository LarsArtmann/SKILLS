# Market Research — Voice of Customer for a Product With No Customers

Find the exact words buyers already use before writing one line of copy or
choosing a channel. Copy that invents its own language converts worse than copy
that mirrors the market's. Copyhackers' whole methodology rests on this: research
is the largest phase of conversion copywriting — "if [research] is not the biggest
part of the work, 99% of the time it means you're doing it wrong" (Joanna Wiebe,
[copyhackers.com/3-part-conversion-copywriting-process-for-newbies](https://copyhackers.com/3-part-conversion-copywriting-process-for-newbies/)).

## Signal-strength ladder (use the highest rung available)

| Rung | Source                                                | Why it beats the rung below               |
| ---- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| 1    | Sales-call / demo transcripts (yours or competitors') | Real buying language, spoken under intent |
| 2    | Customer interviews                                   | Depth, but self-reported                  |
| 3    | Support tickets, win/loss notes, live-chat logs       | Unfiltered pain vocabulary                |
| 4    | Onboarding/thank-you surveys                          | Structured, but prompted                  |
| 5    | Review mining (competitors' reviews)                  | Available at day zero — the 0→1 default   |

## Review mining — the zero-customer recipe (Copyhackers)

Answer four questions in order before mining:

1. Who is the target audience?
2. What outcomes do they want?
3. What solutions are they using today? (including spreadsheets, agencies, nothing)
4. Where do those solutions live online? → mine THERE.

Rules:

- Mine similar/adjacent products and books about the problem, not only direct
  competitors. "It's about your prospects and the outcome they're seeking. It's
  not about your product."
- Sites: Amazon reviews, Reddit, G2/Capterra, App Store, GitHub issues of
  competitor tools, niche forums, YouTube comments, Glassdoor (B2B pain).
- Volume beats precision: Wiebe's rehab-center case read 500+ Amazon reviews of
  six addiction books in ~3 hours and pulled the headline "If you think you need
  rehab, you do" — it beat the control by >400% on button clicks and >20% on
  form submits ([case](https://copyhackers.com/2014/10/amazon-review-mining/)).
- Shortcut dork: `site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "tired of" <keyword>` —
  swap the phrase for "frustrated by", "waste of money", "switched from".
- Harvest into three buckets: exact phrases for pain, exact phrases for desired
  outcome, objections ("I cancelled because..."). Reuse the phrases verbatim on
  the page — 90% of copywriting is lightly editing the customer's own words.

## Sales Safari — where crowds already gather (30x500, Amy Hoy & Alex Hillman)

Passive observation of communities (subreddits, Slack/Discord groups, forums,
GitHub discussions) collecting four things:

| Quadrant        | Collect                                                                                       |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Pain            | First- and second-level pain (what they complain about, and why it matters enough to discuss) |
| Jargon          | Their exact vocabulary, abbreviations, tool names                                             |
| Worldview       | What they believe about the problem, what they blame                                          |
| Recommendations | What they tell each other to use — your real competitor list                                  |

Sell to people who ALREADY pay for solutions in this space. "Validation is
backwards" (30x500): don't ask the market to validate your idea; find pain
people already pay to fix, then build the better answer.

## Interviewing — Mom Test rules (Rob Fitzpatrick)

People lie politely, especially about future intent. Rules:

1. Talk about THEIR life, not your idea.
2. Ask about specifics in the PAST, not generalities about the future.
3. Listen more than you talk.

Replace `Would you buy this?` (useless — polite yes) with:

- "Tell me about the last time this problem cost you time or money."
- "What have you tried already? What did it cost? Why did you stop?"
- "Walk me through how you handle this today, step by step."

A real problem leaves a trail of failed workarounds. If they cannot name a
recent specific instance, the problem is not real for them. Commitment
advances (a scheduled pilot, a forward payment) are the only signals that beat
words.

## Survey questions that produce copy (Copyhackers)

Run post-signup / post-purchase, open-ended, max 2 questions at a time:

- "What was going on in your life that brought you to sign up today?" →
  trigger + stakes, in their words.
- "What almost stopped you from signing up?" → objections → FAQ section.
- "What did you try before this, and why didn't it work?" → alternatives +
  your differentiation.
- "What would you tell a friend on the fence about this?" → testimonial seeds.

## Awareness stage — diagnose before writing (Eugene Schwartz, _Breakthrough Advertising_)

Awareness = how much the visitor knows. It sets where the page starts.

| Stage            | Visitor state                     | Headline must lead with                                             |
| ---------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1 Unaware        | Doesn't know the problem exists   | Identity / call-out of the market; never the product                |
| 2 Problem aware  | Feels the pain, no solution known | The problem, in their words ("If you've tried everything for X...") |
| 3 Solution aware | Knows solutions exist, not yours  | Your MECHANISM — the new way you deliver the outcome                |
| 4 Product aware  | Knows you, not convinced          | Proof: numbers, testimonials, comparison                            |
| 5 Most aware     | Ready to buy                      | The offer: price, bonus, CTA                                        |

Diagnose from three signals:

1. **Search query** — "why is my X broken" = problem aware; "X vs Y" /
   "X pricing" = solution/most aware.
2. **Traffic source** — cold social prospecting skews problem aware; search skews
   solution aware; your email list / launch audience is product/most aware.
   Cold ≠ unaware: a cold Google searcher who typed a pain query is problem aware.
3. **On-site behavior** — repeat pricing-page visits = product aware with one
   unresolved hesitation.

When signals disagree, write for the EARLIER stage: over-educating costs
seconds, under-educating costs the visitor.

## Market sophistication — how tired is your niche (Schwartz)

Sophistication is market-level fatigue, and it sets what the page argues:

| Stage | Market state                 | Winning argument                                          |
| ----- | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Nothing like you exists      | State the claim plainly                                   |
| 2     | Claim is repeated everywhere | Enlarge it with numbers/speed                             |
| 3     | Claims are disbelieved       | Introduce a named MECHANISM (most markets sit here)       |
| 4     | Mechanism copied             | Better/faster mechanism, or narrow to a specific audience |
| 5     | Cynical, saturated           | Sell identity — who they become                           |

Diagnose: read the top 10 competitors' headlines. Shared dead words
("AI-powered", "all-in-one") = stage 2-3+. Everyone selling a branded framework
= stage 3-4. Count how many alternatives a Googler sees before reaching you.

Core principle, in Schwartz's words: copy cannot create desire — it can only
"take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist in the hearts of
millions of people, and focus those already existing desires onto a particular
product." If the desire is not already present in the market, no page fixes
that; go find a hungrier crowd (Halbert's "starving crowd": market selection
beats copy quality every time).
