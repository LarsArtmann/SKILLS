# Positioning & the Ideal Customer

Positioning decides what the visitor compares you to before they read a single
benefit. April Dunford: "Positioning defines how our product is different and
better than alternatives for a particular set of customers. Strong positioning
is the foundation of great marketing and sales"
([aprildunford.com](https://aprildunford.com/)). Default positioning (vague
"all-in-one platform for everyone") is why pages read like everyone else's.

## Dunford's positioning process (*Obviously Awesome*, 2019)

Run the five steps in order; each feeds the next:

1. **List competitive alternatives** — what your best customers would use if
   you didn't exist. Include "do nothing", spreadsheets, scripts, agencies, and
   the status quo — not just named competitors. If you can't name real
   alternatives, you may not have a real market.
2. **Extract unique attributes** — what you (and only you) have that the
   alternatives lack. Cluster attributes into 2-3 value themes (capability →
   benefit). Attributes nobody else claims beat feature lists everyone shares.
3. **Map value to target characteristics** — for each value theme, name WHO
   cares about it most and under what circumstances. Value that matters to
   everyone matters to no one on the page.
4. **Choose a market category** — the frame visitors use to place you
   ("email tool for X", "CI for Y"). Hot categories get attention but instant
   comparison; boring categories get slow attention but stand alone. A
   micro-SaaS usually wins by narrowing the category ("status pages for
   agencies"), not inventing one.
5. **Write the positioning statement** — fill it in, keep it one paragraph:

```
For <target customer> who <situation/trigger>,
<product> is a <market category>
that <key benefit / reason to believe>.
Unlike <main alternative>,
<product> <primary differentiation>.
```

Revisit every 6-12 months — the customers who gave you traction are often not
the ones who take you further (ProductLed,
[target customer profile guide](https://productled.com/blog/how-to-create-your-target-customer-profile)).

## Target profile → ICP (ProductLed 5-step framework)

At 0→1 you segment by hand, not by analytics: define criteria (industry, team
size, role), analyze which segments show pain + budget, overlay demographics
captured at signup (Shopify-style one-question steps), write a one-page profile
per segment (name, photo, description, key data), and re-check it every 6-12
months. Distinguish: target customer profile (several viable segments) →
ideal customer profile (the best-fit segment) → buyer persona (the individual
you message).

## The value proposition line

Peep Laja's test (CXL): a value prop answers four things in one breath —
what the product is, for whom, the primary benefit, and why you instead of
alternatives. Uniqueness test: "the only \<category\> that \<unique
attribute\> for \<segment\>". Quantify whenever honest: "cut month-end close
from 10 days to 3" beats "faster close" (specificity principle, Claude
Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising*: numbers beat adjectives, and every claim
needs a "because").

Where it lives on the page: headline = outcome/who, subhead = mechanism/how
(see [./sales-page-anatomy.md](./sales-page-anatomy.md)).

## Micro-SaaS defaults

- Narrow the ICP until the page can say "for \<specific role/segment\>" — a
  page for everyone converts for no one (SaaS median 3.8%; specific pages do
  multiples of that — Unbounce 2024 benchmark).
- Name the alternative explicitly ("Tired of cobbling spreadsheets + Zapier?")
  when your real competitor is the manual status quo.
- Your differentiation at 0→1 is often not features: it is speed of support,
  founder access, niche fit, or price model. That is legitimate positioning
  material.
